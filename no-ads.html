A prayer times app with no ads and no tracking

Because worship shouldn't come with an advertisement.

If you are here because an Islamic app you trusted filled up with adverts, or put the Qur'an behind a subscription, you are not imagining it and you are not alone.

What the reviews actually say

We read the public App Store reviews of five widely-used prayer apps — 14,057 reviews across 12 storefronts, pulled in August 2026. Most people rate these apps highly. But among the 1,985 reviews rated one or two stars, 867 — about 44% — mention adverts or paywalls. No other topic in those unhappy reviews comes close, and they arrive from storefronts across the Gulf, South Asia, Europe and North America alike.

How we counted, and what this does not say. Apple publishes a customer-review feed for every app. We pulled it in August 2026 for five prayer apps across 12 storefronts, kept the reviews rated 1–2 stars, and counted how many mention adverts or paywalls, in English or Arabic.

The honest limits of that. Counting a mention is not the same as proving a cause — we can say these reviews raise ads, not that ads are the sole reason each person was unhappy. The 44% is of the unhappy reviews, not of all users: 1–2 star reviews are about 14% of the sample, so this is roughly 6% of everything we read. Apple's feed returns recent reviews first and caps at about 10 pages per storefront, so this is a recent-weighted retrievable sample, not a representative survey; the median matched review is from late 2025, and 783 of the 867 fall within two years. A storefront is a country, not a city. And these are five apps we selected as close competitors — we are not claiming a usage ranking.

And it is not evenly spread, which matters. Two of the five apps we looked at have almost no advertising complaints at all — one has none. The pattern is concentrated in the apps that carry advertising, which is the point rather than a caveat to it. The full per-app breakdown, the exact counting rules and the storefront list are published as review-data.json so you can check the arithmetic yourself instead of taking our word. Competitors are anonymised there as App A–E: the finding is about how ad funding behaves, not about naming rivals.

The part that isn't about money

73 of those unhappy reviews go further than saying ads are annoying. They name what the ads showed them — gambling, music, immodest images — next to the Qur'an. People wrote that they had become wary of opening a prayer app in the masjid because they could not predict what would appear on screen.

That is not a bug any of those apps can simply fix while advertising pays for them: an ad network decides what appears, and the app does not see it first. An app with no ad network attached cannot show you something you would be ashamed of, because there is nothing plugged in that could choose for you.

The honest small print

A few features do reach the internet, and only when you ask for them: searching for a nearby mosque sends your approximate area (rounded to about a kilometre) to an open mapping service; Qur'an audio streams when you play it. None of it comes to us — we run no server — and all of it is listed plainly in our privacy policy. We would rather tell you that than claim a perfect absolute we cannot stand behind.
